Rolling stock: build emergency supplies from things you already use

Count what you have, fill the gaps and keep it moving through everyday use.

By Everyday Japan Lab · Reviewed 2026-09-24 · Research and planning, not product certification

What does “rolling stock” mean here?

It means holding an extra supply of suitable everyday food, using the older items in normal meals and replacing them. Japan’s agriculture ministry explains this approach as part of household food preparedness. It is a public recommendation and a useful planning method; we do not have evidence that every Japanese household follows it. [2]

The practical problem is familiar: a box bought for emergencies can be forgotten, while an everyday pantry may have too little reserve. Rotation connects the reserve to an existing shopping routine. It can also reveal foods that the household would not actually eat before a stressful situation occurs.

Choose a planning horizon deliberately

Japan’s Cabinet Office advises at least three days of household supplies and preferably a week. Local hazards, access and public guidance may call for a different period. The calculator accepts a chosen number of days rather than presenting one quantity as sufficient for every disaster. [1]

A smaller plan that fits the home can be a starting point, not proof of complete readiness. Review the needs of people who are away during the day, visitors, pets and anyone who depends on particular supplies. Keep individualized health or dietary advice with the appropriate professional.

Separate four decisions

DecisionQuestion to resolve
WaterWhich local guideline applies, and what uses does its allowance include?
FoodCan the household eat and prepare it during the likely outage?
SanitationWhat can be used if water or sewer service is unavailable?
Storage and rotationCan supplies be reached, inspected and replaced without blocking routes?

Count the reserve you already own

Our calculator uses people × days × a daily allowance, then subtracts usable stock. A water deficit is rounded up to whole containers of the chosen capacity. Meal portions and toilet uses are counted separately. A package labelled “ten servings” should not automatically be entered as ten complete individual meals.

Count only items that can serve the intended purpose. Food that requires unavailable cooking equipment is different from ready-to-eat food. A water container with an unknown condition is different from a verified usable supply. The arithmetic cannot judge those qualities for you.

Do not transfer water guidelines without their scope

The Japanese planning reference uses approximately 3 litres of drinking water per person per day. CDC’s US guidance uses at least one US gallon per person per day and discusses several uses, including drinking and cooking. These are not identical allowances with different unit labels. The tool offers both as labelled starting points and permits a custom rate. [1, 3]

Include a sanitation plan

Japan’s disaster-toilet materials use five toilet visits per person per day as a planning assumption. Actual needs vary. The calculator returns uses to supply; one use does not necessarily equal one complete package or one particular product’s bag. Confirm the product instructions, waste storage and local disposal guidance. It does not tell you whether a damaged sewer is safe to use. [4]

Make the routine easy to keep

  1. List food and supplies the household already uses and can store appropriately.
  2. Record quantities and the nearest date needing attention.
  3. Place older suitable stock where it will be used first.
  4. Replace used reserve items during normal shopping.
  5. Review the plan when the household, season or local guidance changes.

This is a quantity and shopping tool, not a complete emergency plan, nutritional assessment or assurance that supplies will be adequate. Local official instructions take priority during an incident.

Sources and scope

Primary references checked 2026-09-24. Manufacturer statements are identified as such.

  1. Cabinet Office Japan — Public preparedness guidance
    Three days minimum and preferably a week; roughly 3 L drinking water per person per day. Guidance is not observed adoption.
  2. MAFF Japan — Household food-stock guidance
    Rotate familiar foods; recommendation does not establish household compliance.
  3. CDC — Public emergency-water guidance
    At least one US gallon per person per day for three days; aim for two weeks if possible. Scope differs from Japan's drinking-water figure.
  4. METI Japan — Disaster toilet working-group material
    Five uses per person per day is a planning assumption; individual and product needs vary.
