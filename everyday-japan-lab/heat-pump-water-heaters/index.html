Heat-pump water heaters: what Japan’s EcoCute experience can teach

Compare a water-heating system against your household’s demand, installation conditions and local energy prices.

By Everyday Japan Lab · Reviewed 2026-09-24 · Research and planning, not product certification

What is the useful idea?

A heat-pump water heater uses electricity to move heat into stored water. Its heat output can exceed its electrical input because it also draws heat from its surroundings. This differs from an electric resistance element that converts electrical energy directly into heat. The ratio is often described as a coefficient of performance, or COP. [3]

For a household, the question is practical: can the system supply enough hot water at an acceptable installed and operating cost, in the available space? A high efficiency figure alone cannot answer that question.

What is established in Japan?

EcoCute is a Japanese category associated with CO2-refrigerant heat-pump water heating. The Japanese industry association reported cumulative shipments exceeding ten million in March 2025. IEA’s 2026 market review describes Japan as a mature heat-pump market and discusses CO2 water heating. Shipment history supports an established industry, not a claim that every household uses EcoCute. [1, 2]

The transferable lesson is to consider hot-water storage, heat-pump performance and household demand together. It is not an instruction to import a Japanese 100 V or 200 V appliance. A locally approved system with service support is the relevant comparison.

Do not confuse different system layouts

LayoutWhat to investigate
Outdoor heat-pump unit with separate storage tankOutdoor siting, climate rating, noise, pipe runs, freeze protection and tank location
Integrated indoor heat-pump tankRoom air volume or ducting, airflow, condensate disposal and interaction with space conditioning
Resistance backup within a heat-pump systemWhen it operates, what demand it supports and its effect on seasonal energy use

US ENERGY STAR guidance for integrated units discusses installation conditions such as air supply and the surrounding space. Those requirements should not be copied wholesale onto a split CO2 system. Read the manual and local installation guidance for the system actually under consideration. [4, 5]

Build a comparison from useful hot water

Estimate the daily volume of delivered warm water and the temperature rise from the cold inlet. The calculator converts that demand into useful thermal energy, adds an explicit heat-loss allowance, and divides the heat-pump share by an assumed seasonal COP. Resistance backup is counted separately at COP 1.

“Delivered temperature” means the mixed water at the point of use in this model. It is not a recommended tank setpoint. Tank temperature, hygiene control, scald protection and mixing arrangements must follow local requirements and the manufacturer. This distinction prevents a volume of mixed shower water from being mistaken for an equal volume of hotter stored water.

Why the cheapest result may not be the best decision

A low modelled bill does not include every cost. Consider installation work, available tank sizes, peak demand, recovery time, service access, noise, warranties and repair arrangements. An indoor unit’s effect on space heating or cooling may also matter. The calculator excludes those interactions; a local assessment can investigate them.

Use the same service boundary for the baseline. A gas price per billing unit must be converted into the energy unit used in the tool, and the efficiency must describe the same useful-heat boundary. Fixed charges are not included. Removing a gas appliance does not automatically remove the household’s entire gas standing charge.

A useful quote request

  1. Give the installer your household’s hot-water pattern and any unusually concentrated demand.
  2. Document the proposed unit and tank locations, electrical supply and service access.
  3. Ask which performance data apply to your climate and selected operating mode.
  4. Ask when backup heating operates and how the quoted capacity meets peak demand.
  5. Compare full installed cost and the assumptions behind projected energy use.
