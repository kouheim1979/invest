COP, heat loss and hot-water cost: understand the calculator

Follow the units and change one assumption at a time.

By Everyday Japan Lab · Reviewed 2026-09-24 · Research and planning, not product certification

Start with the water’s temperature rise

For an approximate household model, take water density as 1 kg per litre and specific heat capacity as 4.186 kJ per kg per °C. Heating V litres through a temperature rise ΔT requires V × 4.186 × ΔT ÷ 3,600 kWh of useful heat. These are physical modelling assumptions, not appliance test results.

At 200 litres per day and a 25 °C rise, this is about 5.8 kWh of useful heat per day. This is an illustrative demand, not a national average.

Define the heat-loss boundary

The tool adds a user-entered percentage to useful draw heat. A 15% input means modelled heat generation is 1.15 times useful draw heat. It does not mean that 15% of input electricity disappears, and it is not a measured loss rate for any product. A real installation may need a different model, especially at very low hot-water use.

Separate heat-pump and resistance heat

Electricity = modelled heat × [(1 − resistance heat fraction) ÷ seasonal COP + resistance heat fraction]

The resistance share is a fraction of heat supplied, not a fraction of operating hours. The heat-pump COP input should represent the selected performance boundary. If published seasonal data already include backup heating or tank losses, do not add those effects again. Consult the rating’s definition or set the additional allowances appropriately.

Compare the baseline honestly

Baseline energy equals the same modelled heat divided by baseline efficiency. Multiply each energy input by its own price per kWh. A negative saving means the heat-pump scenario costs more under those assumptions. The tool deliberately allows that outcome.

Simple payback divides a positive additional upfront cost by a positive annual saving. It ignores financing, maintenance, future tariffs, replacement and residual value. If the saving is zero or negative, or no extra cost is provided, it reports no payback rather than inventing a break-even date.

What the model leaves out

It does not simulate hourly weather, draw patterns, storage recovery, solar self-consumption, demand charges, plumbing circulation or interactions with space conditioning. COP sensitivity at 2, 3 and 4 is a scenario comparison, not a promised operating range. Manufacturer performance data and local design review are the next sources to use.
