A rice cup is a unit, not a serving

Japanese rice-cooker measurements commonly use a 180 mL cup of uncooked rice. It is different from many kitchen measuring cups. Two such cups measure 360 mL; that is a volume, not 360 grams and not two portions of cooked rice. Zojirushi's specifications illustrate this convention. [2]

Use the marked millilitre scale on a measuring jug if available. If using another cup, check its stated volume first; the helper below converts this fixed batch to your cup size. It does not resize the recipe or predict cooked portions.

The method, with cues you can see

  1. Rinse and soak. Put rinsed rice with the measured water in the pan. Allow about 30 minutes in summer or 60 in winter.
  2. Cover and heat. Use medium-high heat until bubbling and steam appear.
  3. Reduce to low. Cook for roughly 10–12 minutes, until free water and active bubbling have subsided.
  4. Turn off the heat. Keep covered for 5–10 minutes.
  5. Fluff. Open carefully and gently loosen the rice.

Timing and water follow the named reference method, not a universal rule. Follow your cookware and hob instructions, including compatibility with the heat source. A different pan, lid or batch may need a different recipe. [1]

What transfers from everyday Japan?

Japan's agriculture ministry describes absorption cooking as the usual Japanese approach: water is measured before heating, and no surplus is drained at the end. It also explains that insufficient soaking can leave a firm centre because water and heat do not reach the grain evenly. This is context for the method, not evidence that most Japanese households cook in a pan. [3]

The useful idea is a repeatable sequence: measure, allow absorption, control the heat, then rest. Write down your rice, pan, water and result. If you need to adapt a reliable recipe, change one variable at a time so the next attempt tells you something.

When the result is not what you expected

Firm centres
Check that you used the intended rice and completed the soaking step. A timer measures elapsed time, not the state of the grain. [3]
Too wet or too dry
First check the measuring units and whether the recipe matches your pan. Record the actual water amount and result before changing the next batch.
Scorching or a strong burning smell
Turn off the heat and investigate. Do not keep heating simply because a countdown has time remaining.

Pan or rice cooker?

A pan lets you try this method with suitable cookware you may already own, but you must manage the heat and timing. A rice cooker can be useful when you want an automatic programme or other functions; check the exact model rather than assuming all products have the same features. Neither approach is automatically cheaper, tastier or more energy-efficient. We have not run a comparative cooking test.

Conversion, timer behaviour and limits

Volume conversion: cupfuls = required volume in mL ÷ your cup's marked capacity in mL. For the example 250 mL cup, dry rice is 360 ÷ 250 = 1.44 cups and water is 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6 cups. Results are rounded to three decimal places; fractional cupfuls are approximate to measure. The 50–500 mL input range is this tool's validation limit, not a cookware limit.

No water-scaling model: this page retains the reference two-rice-cup batch. It does not multiply cooking time by rice quantity, convert rice volume to weight, or infer capacity from a pan's diameter.

Timer: remaining seconds = the rounded-up positive difference between a target timestamp and the current time. Time spent away from the tab is counted when you return; reminders may arrive late. The boil step is an elapsed clock, not a predicted boiling time. No step advances automatically. You can confirm a step already completed; changing the timer does not control the hob. A changed device clock can affect the displayed time.
